# Workout planner: evidence audit (November 2026)

What the trainer assumes when it builds a session, what the research says, and what changed. Plain language; citations are the ones in the code comments.

## Summary

Most of the planner already matched the literature: big lifts first, 10–20 hard sets per muscle per week, stopping 1–3 reps short of failure, rest of 2–3 min on big lifts and about 90 s on small ones, RPE-based load suggestions and a small day-to-day effort swing from recovery. Five things changed:

1. **Sessions now fit the time you picked.** Sets are trimmed (down to 2 per exercise, main lifts last) until the estimate fits, instead of overrunning.
2. **Progression handles big jumps.** A jump of up to 10% is taken as soon as you reach the top of the rep range (double progression). When the next weight is a bigger jump (light dumbbells, coarse stacks), the planner asks for 2 extra reps first and tells you so.
3. **Stretched-position exercises are preferred** where the evidence is clear: overhead triceps extensions over pushdowns, seated over lying leg curls, standing over seated calf raises.
4. **Time estimates use the real rest timer defaults** for your training style instead of fixed numbers.
5. **Citation comments** were added or corrected next to every rule (order, volume, effort, rest, autoregulation, RPE accuracy, recovery times).

## Assumption by assumption

| Area | What the code does | What the research says | Verdict | Change |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Exercise order | Multi-joint lifts first | Early-session lifts gain more strength; growth doesn't depend on order (Nunes et al., Eur J Sport Sci 2021; ACSM 2009) | Supported | Citation added |
| Sets and reps by goal | Strength 3–6 reps heavy; muscle 6–15; balanced 6–12; endurance 10–20 | Load ranges from ~30% to 80%+ 1RM all build muscle; heavier is better for strength (Schoenfeld et al., 2017, 2021; Lopez et al., 2021) | Supported | None |
| Effort (RPE/RIR) | Big lifts 2–3 reps in reserve, small lifts closer to failure | Failure adds little for growth and nothing for strength; closer to failure helps hypertrophy more than strength (Refalo et al., 2024; Robinson et al., Sports Med 2024) | Supported | Citation updated |
| Recovery → effort | RPE 6.5 / 7.5 / 8 by recovery band, never skips | Autoregulation works for strength (Larsen et al., PeerJ 2021); HRV-guided benefits are small and mainly in endurance (Granero-Gallegos 2020; Manresa-Rocamora 2021) | Supported, kept small | Citation added |
| Rest | 2–3 min big lifts, ~90 s small | Singer et al., 2024; Schoenfeld et al., 2016; ACSM 2009 | Supported | Citation tidied |
| Weekly volume | 10–20 sets/muscle "good", 20+ "high if you recover", under 10 "low" | Dose–response with diminishing returns; ~4 sets already gives most of the strength gain (Schoenfeld 2017; Pelland et al., Sports Med 2026; ACSM 2026) | Supported | Comment updated |
| Frequency | Rotates focus so each muscle is hit ~2×/week | Frequency matters mostly through volume (Schoenfeld et al., 2016, 2019) | Supported | None |
| Progression | Next jump once top of range is hit at target effort | ACSM: add 2–10% when 1–2 reps over target are reached | Tweak | Big-jump rule (>10% needs +2 reps) |
| Session length | Fixed exercise count by minutes; estimate could overrun | Time-efficient training: fewer sets of big lifts, supersets (Iversen et al., Sports Med 2021) | Wrong (overran) | `fit` trims sets to the time |
| Exercise choice | Catalog order | Long-muscle-length work grows more: triceps overhead (Maeo 2023), seated leg curl (Maeo 2021), standing calf raise (Kinoshita 2023) | Tweak | Catalog reordered |
| RPE accuracy | Up to 4 reps in reserve counted in e1RM | Lifters misjudge by ~1 rep, worse far from failure (Halperin et al., Sports Med 2022) | Supported | Citation added |
| Muscle recovery | 48–72 h model; soreness overrides | Soreness tracks damage poorly (Nosaka et al., 2002) | Model, not measurement | Comment says so |
| Deloads | Suggested on high volume + poor recovery | Consensus: planned or reactive, both fine (Bell et al., 2023) | Supported | None |
| Warm-up | 5 min allowance; no ramp sets | Ramp sets help performance (Ribeiro et al., 2020) | Gap | Future: warm-up calculator rounded to plates |

## Open items (done in roadmap Phase 4, November 2026)
- Warm-up ramp calculator (2–3 sets at 50/70/85% rounded to your plates): `WarmUp` in AppCore, shown in the builder and prefilled as "W" sets (Ribeiro 2020; Fradkin 2010; ACSM 2009). The 50/70/85% pattern is coaching practice, not a tested optimum.
- Supersets for short sessions, instead of only trimming sets: `Supersets` pairs non-competing movements once sets are down to two, before any exercise is dropped; paired sets are estimated at ~60% of their separate time (Iversen 2021; Weakley 2017). Strength style keeps big lifts as straight sets.
- Training blocks (4–6 weeks, volume ramp, planned deload) under the HRV guidance: `TrainingBlock` (strength/balanced 5 weeks, muscle 6, sets ×1.0 → 1.25/1.4 then 0.6, RIR 3 → 1 then 3; Williams 2017, Bell 2023, Pelland 2026). A week that starts with 7-day HRV below normal repeats the previous week's sets; a low-recovery day still lowers the RPE inside the block. Stamina/health goals keep steady weeks.

## Pre-workout warm-up

Rebuilt November 2026 (research in the roadmap's warm-up report). Settings › Training › Warm-up offers four tiers (`WarmUpRoutine.Tier`), default **Ramp sets only**, remembered per focus from the planner: Off · Ramp sets only · Short (~5 min) · Full (~8–10 min). The ramp sets of the first lift (`WarmUp`) are the part with evidence; the movement steps (`WarmUpRoutine`) are a short, fixed, rep-based list chosen by the first two movement patterns of the session, with one timed "raise" step. What the evidence supports, and what it doesn't:

| Question | What the research says | What the app does |
|---|---|---|
| What matters most? | The specific warm-up: ramp sets of the lift itself. In 40 trained men, specific sets at 40 % + 80 % of the training load (bench) or a single 80 % set (squat) optimised force output; "a few reps at low loads is not enough" (Ribeiro et al., Int J Environ Res Public Health 2020, trial). A systematic review of 11 studies found results "not consensual", with specific warm-up alone often best for maximal strength, strongest when the sets were close to the working load (Ribeiro, Neves et al., Motricidade 2021, systematic review). A heavier last ramp set beat a lighter one for volume over three sets to failure (two 2024 crossovers, 15 men each; one lab mostly — "likely", not settled). For submaximal hypertrophy sets, general vs specific vs no warm-up made no difference (Ribeiro et al., Percept Mot Skills 2014). | Ramp sets are the default tier and run at every tier except Off. The UI calls the movement benefit "small". Note: the earlier "Ribeiro et al., JSCR 2020, review" citation was wrong; it is the IJERPH 2020 trial plus the Motricidade 2021 review. |
| Does a general raise help? | Mechanisms are mainly muscle temperature plus metabolic and neural effects; much practice grew by trial and error, and the benefit decays during the gap before the first effort (McGowan, Pyne, Thompson & Rattray, Sports Med 2015, narrative review). A 20-min bike raise improved leg-press 1RM by ~8 % (Abad et al., J Strength Cond Res 2011, 13 trained) but that is a 1RM test and far too long for a gym session. Muscle temperature falls ~0.1 °C/min at rest; jump height −3.8 % after 9 min idle (Res Q Exerc Sport 2022). A general warm-up of any kind improved performance in 79 % of criteria across 32 studies, few of them well-run RCTs (Fradkin, Zazryn & Smoliga, J Strength Cond Res 2010, meta-analysis). | One timed raise step: 90 s (Short) or 180 s (Full) of marching, jogging in place, or an easy bike or rower if the gym has one. The routine is capped so the first working set stays within ~10 min: Short ≤ 5 min, Full ≤ 10 min of planned steps (tested). |
| Dynamic drills / mobility | Dynamic stretching gave +1.3 % acute performance, static −3.7 %, PNF −4.4 %; range-of-motion gains fade within ~30 min; no injury data for dynamic stretching (Behm, Blazevich, Kay & McHugh, Appl Physiol Nutr Metab 2016, systematic review / CSEP position stand). Static holds under 30 s cost about 1 %, 60 s or more much more (Kay & Blazevich, Med Sci Sports Exerc 2012). Stretching did not reduce injuries (RR 0.96) while strength training did (RR 0.32) (Lauersen, Bertelsen & Andersen, Br J Sports Med 2014, meta-analysis). Foam rolling: big acute ROM gain, no performance benefit (Wilke et al. 2020). Bench 1RM was unchanged with or without stretching or drills before heavy upper-body lifts. | 2–3 rep-based drills keyed by the first two lifts' movement patterns (squat, hinge, lunge, horizontal press, vertical press, pull), each with one cue and one "why" that claims range of motion, rehearsal or activation only (e.g. "Opens the hips for squat depth"). No static holds. The copy says the benefit is small and mostly feel-based, and never claims injury prevention. |
| Explosive reps before set 1 | Post-activation performance enhancement: small effects (jumps ES 0.29, upper body 0.23; larger with more rest and in stronger lifters) (Seitz & Haff, Sports Med 2016, meta-analysis, 47 studies, n = 1954). Driven by temperature, water content and activation, not twitch potentiation (Blazevich & Babault, Front Physiol 2019). Plyo push-ups before bench raised power at 70 % 1RM (Krzysztofik et al., J Hum Kinet 2020). | Full tier only: a last step of 3 jump squats (lower-body first lift) or 3 plyo push-ups (upper), labelled "after ramp sets", with "the effect is small" in the why line. |
| Order and structure | RAMP: Raise, Activate and Mobilise, Potentiate (Jeffreys, Professional Strength and Conditioning 2007; UKSCA-endorsed; the article was not re-verified in full text). NSCA Essentials ch. 14: general then specific warm-up, dynamic rather than static. Both are coaching practice, not tested lifting protocols; no phase durations are given. | Raise → drills → (Full: activate/mobilise additions, explosive reps) → ramp sets. The drills, doses and the 90/180 s raise are coaching practice; the app does not present them as tested optima. |

Why the first version was rebuilt: every movement had a countdown, so rep drills auto-advanced mid-rep; the round-robin picker across muscle groups gave a grab-bag with no visible link to the day's lifts; the routine was a 5–8 min budget filler, longer than the raise, inverting the evidence weighting; and there was no back navigation. Now: only the raise is timed, drills advance on tap (phone and Watch), every step shows name, dose, one cue and one why, there is a pre-flight overview with "Skip to ramp sets", Back on every step, and the tier is remembered per focus.

Not covered by the evidence above: the exact drills and their rep counts, the 90/180 s raise lengths, and the ~3 s-per-rep time estimate used for the routine's total are coaching practice or planning estimates, not measured values.

## Weekly coverage and flexible weeks (November 2026)

Two linked changes to how the week is planned: the day's session is chosen by which muscles are furthest from the 10–20 hard-set zone (`MuscleCoverage`), and a plan can be a weekly quota on any days instead of fixed weekdays (`WeeklyPlan.Mode.flexible`, `TrainingDayPlanner.flexibleGuidance`).

| Question | What the research says | What the app does |
|---|---|---|
| What is the weekly target per muscle? | Dose–response: about 10–20 hard sets a muscle a week builds the most muscle for most people (Schoenfeld, Ogborn & Krieger, J Sports Sci 2017, meta-analysis); more keeps adding a little with diminishing returns, helper muscles counted as half a set (Pelland et al., Sports Med 2026); ACSM 2026 suggests about 10 for growth. | The green zone on the Hard sets this week card and the muscle map is 10–20 (`MuscleRecovery.State.volume`). `MuscleCoverage` counts the gap to 10 for ten tracked muscles (the card's list without abs) since Monday (`setsThisWeek`, not a rolling 7 days). |
| Does the day a muscle is trained matter? | For equal weekly volume, how the sets are spread over the week makes little difference to growth (Schoenfeld, Grgic & Krieger, J Sports Sci 2019, volume-equated frequency meta-analysis); training a muscle about twice a week looked better than once when volume was not equated (Schoenfeld et al., Sports Med 2016). | The week's total per muscle is the target; the focus moves to whichever session closes the most of the gap. A muscle that is not yet fresh in the 48–72 h recovery model earns no credit and a focus built on a fatigued muscle is skipped, so sessions still spread out. |
| How should the day's focus be chosen? | No trial compares ways of choosing the day's session. | **App choice, said so in code.** Each focus is scored by the lagging sets it would supply (about 18 sets a session shared by its muscles' weights, each counted only up to its gap), highest wins, the usual rotation wins ties. On the week's last session a lagging muscle outside the focus gets a priority movement (`WorkoutPlanner.plan(prioritySlots:)`, placed straight after the compound lifts). The UI says "to catch X up", never promises an outcome. |
| Fixed days or any days? | Fixed days and times are implementation intentions: d = 0.65 across 94 tests (Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006), d = 0.31 for physical activity (Bélanger-Gravel 2013); coping plans predicted maintenance (Sniehotta 2005, from memory). The evidence for flexible scheduling is thinner and indirect: autonomy support and autonomous motivation predict adherence (Teixeira et al. 2012, 66 studies; Ntoumanis et al. 2021, 73 studies, both in behaviour-change.md); HRV-guided training (train when recovered, go easy when not) did as well as or better than fixed plans in small trials (Kiviniemi et al., Eur J Appl Physiol 2007; Javaloyes et al., IJSPP 2019; both cited from memory in strain-science.md, not re-verified here); total weekly activity matters more than its pattern (dos Santos et al., JAMA Intern Med 2022, from memory). No trial has compared flexible with fixed training days directly. | Both modes. Existing plans stay fixed; a first plan starts flexible (the editor's footer says fixed days have the stronger evidence and the user picks). In flexible mode the day's guidance is: trained today → training day; quota met → rest to Monday ("Week done"); low recovery → rest when the week has room, else an easy session (never a skip); every day left needed → "train today to make the week" (gentle, the short version counts); hard day yesterday with room → rest suggested, training allowed; else a training day with the coverage focus. Nothing is ever labelled missed. The consistency score counts sessions against the quota in both modes (it always counted any day; `WeekRecord.make` now uses `sessionTarget`). Reminders in flexible mode fire only on days when the days left are no more than the sessions remaining. |

Not covered by the evidence: the one-day spacing after a hard day, the 18-sets-a-session share model, the order of the flexible rules and the reminder threshold are app choices. The Kiviniemi 2007 and Javaloyes 2019 figures were not re-verified this round; the UI keeps "about" and "suits" wording.
